title: PayPal After Q3 2026: Early Signs of a Turnaround
date: 2026-11-01
summary: Three EPS beats in three quarters under Enrique Lores, costs falling faster than planned and $6 billion a year of buybacks. Branded checkout is still flat, and management's own 2027 outlook implies net income keeps shrinking. Can PayPal reach mid-single-digit transaction-margin growth, and what is the stock worth in 2030 if the multiple never moves?
---
> **Update to our [September thesis](/theses/paypal-priced-for-a-permanent-decline/)**, as of November 1, 2026, using a $57 share price. That pitch set an $85 price target for 2030 assuming zero growth and a fixed $6 billion a year of buybacks. This post re-runs the numbers with the Q3 results and lets the buyback grow with cash flow, which is why the base case below comes out higher. Opinion only, not investment advice.

PayPal's third-quarter results, reported on October 27, were the clearest sign yet that the company is starting to turn around. The business is not fixed, and branded checkout is still growing slowly. But under CEO Enrique Lores, PayPal is getting more of its growth from businesses other than the checkout button. Its costs are coming down faster than planned, and it keeps returning large amounts of cash to shareholders. Below I look at whether that is enough to deliver the growth management is promising, and what the stock is worth if it does.

## Three for three under new management

Lores took over as CEO on March 1, 2026. Since then, PayPal has beaten Wall Street's earnings-per-share estimate in all three quarters he has reported:

| Quarter | Non-GAAP EPS | Consensus | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 2026 | $1.34 | $1.27 | Beat |
| Q2 2026 | $1.38 | $1.28 | Beat; full-year guidance raised |
| Q3 2026 | $1.45 | $1.31 | Beat; full-year guidance raised again |

So far, Lores has guided conservatively and then beaten his own numbers:

- **Full-year EPS guidance:** in February, PayPal guided 2026 non-GAAP EPS to anywhere from a low-single-digit decline to slightly positive versus $5.31 in 2025. It raised that to $5.38 in July and to about $5.60 in October, roughly 5-6% growth. Q4 alone is guided to about $1.43, against a $1.35 consensus and $1.23 a year ago.
- **Q3 EPS:** management had guided Q3 EPS to decline year over year. It grew 8%.
- **Q3 operating expenses:** management guided high-single-digit growth. They grew 1%.

Revenue was the one blemish in Q3: $8.58 billion, up 2%, and below the $8.71 billion consensus.

## What Q3 showed

**Branded checkout has stopped getting worse.** Volume through the PayPal checkout button has grown 1-2% for four straight quarters (1% in Q3). That holds even with a roughly 70 basis point drag from the EU removing its de minimis import tax exemption, which management expects to persist through Q4. U.S. checkout improved for a third straight quarter.

**The playbook is working where it has been applied.** At large merchants where PayPal rolled out its full playbook (new checkout flow, Pay Later placement, joint marketing), it gained relative share of checkout over the last four quarters. PayPal gave no aggregate number, but it did give two examples:

- At a leading U.S. sports entertainment platform, PayPal volume grew more than 40% over six months after the partnership was renewed.
- At a large U.S. omnichannel retailer, Pay Later's share of the merchant's mix went from 7% to 20% after the offer was expanded.

Management underwrites these investments over two to three years, and closer to three for the largest merchants.

**The other businesses are growing quickly:**

- **Venmo:** payment volume grew double digits for the eighth straight quarter, and revenue per active account is also growing double digits. Pay with Venmo volume rose 43%. Venmo debit card monthly active accounts reached 6.7 million, up more than 55%. Only 23% of Venmo customers use its financial-services products so far, so there is a long runway.
- **Buy Now, Pay Later and financial services:** Pay Later volume grew 25%. Financial services (BNPL, credit, debit and merchant lending) now make up more than 20% of transaction margin dollars and are growing double digits.
- **Braintree:** volume grew 10%. The share of merchants buying value-added services such as risk tools and payouts rose to 6% from 5%.

**Costs are being cut faster than planned.** PayPal now expects more than $700 million of run-rate savings exiting 2026, toward at least $1.5 billion of gross savings over two to three years. Headcount is down by more than 2,000, three layers of management are gone, and management roles are down by more than a quarter. Non-transaction operating expenses grew just 1% in Q3.

## Can PayPal grow transaction margin dollars by mid-single digits?

The metric that matters is transaction margin dollars (TM$): revenue minus transaction costs and losses, essentially PayPal's gross profit. Management's medium-term target is sustainable mid-single-digit TM$ growth with "solid double-digit" EPS growth. It says that does not require branded checkout to return to its historical growth rate.

The starting point is lower than the headlines suggest:

- **2026 as a whole:** TM$ is guided to about $15.7 billion, up roughly 1.5% from $15.5 billion in 2025. Excluding interest on customer balances (guided at $14.6 billion), growth is about 2.5%.
- **By quarter:** TM$ grew 1% in Q2 and 2% in Q3 (3% excluding interest), and Q4 is guided "slightly positive."

**A note on interest income.** Interest on customer balances fell about 10% in Q3, to $289 million, because short-term rates are lower than a year ago. But the Fed held rates on October 28 and markets are now pricing a possible hike, not more cuts. If rates stay where they are, this drag fades through 2027 instead of continuing. My base case still assumes it shrinks 5% a year, which is probably conservative.

PayPal reports as a single segment, so the split below is my own estimate. It is built from company disclosures and reconciled to the 2025 actuals and the 2026 guide. One important point: branded checkout volume growing 1-2% does not mean branded checkout *TM$* is growing 1-2%. PayPal is spending on co-marketing and merchant incentives, and that compresses the take rate. My estimate is that branded TM$ is roughly flat today.

| Business (my estimates) | 2026E TM$ | Share | Growing now (est.) | Bear | Base (today's rates) | Guidance case |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Branded checkout | $7.6B | 48% | ~0% | −2% | 0% | +1.5% |
| Other PayPal (P2P, cross-border, credit & financial services) | $5.1B | 32% | ~+3.5% | +2% | +3.5% | +9% |
| Venmo | $1.0B | 6% | ~+15% | +10% | +15% | +15% |
| Braintree / PSP | $0.9B | 6% | ~+8% | +5% | +8% | +12% |
| Interest on customer balances | $1.1B | 7% | ~−10% | −8% | −5% | −5% |
| **Total TM$ growth** | **$15.7B** | | **~1.5-2%** | **~0%** | **~2-3%** | **~5%** |

What the math says:

- **At today's rates, TM$ grows about 2-3% a year.** That is above 2026's 1.5% mainly because the drag from interest income should ease. It is healthy, but well short of mid-single digits.
- **The guidance case needs real acceleration.** To reach ~5% with branded checkout at 1.5%, the growth businesses (Other PayPal, Venmo and Braintree combined) need to grow about 10% a year, up from roughly 6% today. On top of that, branded TM$ has to move from flat to +1.5%.
- **The heaviest lift is in "Other PayPal,"** which has to go from about 3.5% to about 9%. That depends on financial services keeping a double-digit pace while the slower pieces of the bucket hold up.

So the target is achievable, but it is not a "couple of points" away. The growth engines need to roughly double their combined growth rate, which is exactly what the reinvested cost savings are meant to fund. That makes 2027 and 2028 the key tests.

## What about operating expenses?

Non-transaction operating expenses grew just 1% in Q3, but that is not a run rate:

- They grew about 8% in Q1.
- Full-year 2026 is guided to mid-single-digit growth, and Q4 to flat to low-single-digit growth.
- The CFO explicitly cautioned against annualizing the Q3 number, because PayPal plans to reinvest a significant share of the savings and step up investment in 2027 in products, marketing and specialized sales teams.

I assume opex grows 3% a year from 2027 to 2030. If the $1.5 billion of gross savings lands and roughly half is reinvested, net opex growth closer to 2% is plausible. I don't count on that.

## From TM$ to EPS: the buyback engine

This is where capital allocation comes in. PayPal plans to buy back about $6 billion of stock in 2026, on at least $6.5 billion of adjusted free cash flow. It has bought back about $6 billion (roughly 117 million shares) over the last twelve months, and diluted shares were down 10.6% year over year in Q3, to 858 million. At about 10x earnings, sending roughly 100% of free cash flow to buybacks and dividends retires around 11% of the share count a year.

Lores has done this before. As CEO of HP from late 2019, he turned down a hostile bid from Xerox in 2020, launched a roughly $15 billion buyback plan, and later committed to returning 100% of free cash flow to shareholders. HP's diluted share count fell about 37% during his tenure. There is a clear parallel at PayPal: in 2026, its board turned down a $60.50-per-share approach from Stripe and Advent International as too low, choosing to back its own plan.

The key insight is simple. At 10x earnings, if the multiple holds, a shareholder earns roughly the free cash flow yield (about 13%) plus whatever net income grows. Growth is not needed for a low-teens return. What growth does is make it much more likely that the multiple holds.

## Valuation: what PayPal is worth in 2030 with no multiple expansion

**Assumptions (2027-2030):**

- **Starting point:** share price of $57 and 2026E non-GAAP EPS of $5.60, which gives a P/E of 10.2x.
- **Multiple:** P/E held at 10.2x. No multiple expansion in any case, and the share price rises with EPS.
- **Buybacks:** funded from adjusted free cash flow at the 2026 ratio (about 1.33x non-GAAP net income), minus a $0.56 dividend. Because the price rises with EPS, each dollar of buyback retires fewer shares over time.
- **Opex:** grows 3% a year.
- **Tax rate:** 19.5%.
- **Stock-based compensation:** about 10 million shares of dilution a year. SBC is already expensed inside PayPal's non-GAAP EPS.
- **Return:** IRR measured from today to the end of 2030, including dividends.

**The four cases:**

- **Bear:** branded checkout loses share and its TM$ shrinks 2% a year, while the growth engines slow.
- **Base:** every business keeps growing at today's rate.
- **Bull:** management delivers mid-single-digit TM$ growth, with branded checkout at a conservative 1.5%.
- **Bull + Ads:** the bull case plus PayPal Ads reaching half of Uber's ad business, about $1 billion of revenue by 2030. Uber's ad business passed a $2 billion run rate at the end of 2025. I assume a 40% operating margin, much lower than a mature ads business would earn.

| | Bear | Base | Bull (guidance) | Bull + Ads |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TM$ growth (2026-30 CAGR) | ~0% | 2.5% | 5.4% | 6.7% |
| Net income growth (CAGR) | −4.6% | +1.6% | +8.8% | +10.1% |
| Share count change per year | ~−10% | ~−11% | ~−11% | ~−11% |
| EPS growth (CAGR) | 6% | 13.5% | 22% | 24% |
| 2030 EPS | $7.08 | $9.28 | $12.47 | $13.10 |
| 2030 value at 10.2x | $72 | $94 | $127 | $133 |
| Upside from $57 | +26% | +66% | +123% | +134% |
| IRR incl. dividends | ~7% | ~14% | ~22% | ~23% |

What this shows:

- **Even the bear case doesn't lose money if the multiple holds.** Net income shrinks every year, but buybacks keep EPS growing about 6% a year. The real risk is that a business with shrinking profits gets re-rated lower. At 8x, the bear case returns about 1% a year.
- **The base case already clears a 13-14% IRR.** It needs no acceleration, only today's growth rates, a stable multiple, and the buyback program. Opex is the biggest swing factor: at 2% growth the base IRR rises to about 15.5%, and at 4% it falls to about 12%.
- **Hitting guidance roughly doubles the stock by 2030.** Mid-single-digit TM$ growth plus 3% opex growth turns into about 9% net income growth. With buybacks on top, EPS grows at more than 20% a year, all at today's multiple.
- **Ads are a small cherry.** Even at half of Uber's scale, ads add only about 1 point of IRR. They are real optionality, but not the thesis.

### The timing problem: management's 2027 outlook

Management is targeting 2027 non-GAAP EPS growth similar to 2026, about 5-6%, because it plans to step up investment. With the share count falling about 11% a year, **that implies net income falls another ~5% in 2027**. My model shows faster 2027 growth than that in every case except the bear case.

If 2027 EPS only grows 6%, the 2030 endpoints above require the following three years to do the heavy lifting: about 16% EPS growth a year from 2028 to 2030 in the base case, and about 28% a year in the bull case. The base case still looks reachable. The bull case needs the 2027 investment to pay off quickly and visibly. For context, management's own medium-term framing is "solid double-digit" EPS growth, so the bull case's 22% a year sits well above what PayPal itself is promising. The IRR depends on where the business ends up in 2030, but the path will be slower and more back-end loaded than these numbers suggest.

## Margin of safety: what I'm not counting

- **Reinvested savings.** The cost program targets at least $1.5 billion of gross run-rate savings. A large share is going into financial services, Venmo monetization, specialized Braintree sales teams, and taking the U.S. merchant playbook to Europe in 2027. I hold opex at 3% growth and give no credit for the extra growth this spending could buy.
- **Checkout share gains.** At merchants with the full playbook, PayPal's share of checkout is improving, and the examples above are material: +40% volume at one partner, Pay Later share going from 7% to 20% at another. Only a small part of the merchant base has the full playbook so far. I assume none of this spreads beyond 1.5% branded growth, even in the bull case.
- **Interest rates.** If rates stay flat or rise, interest on customer balances stops shrinking. I still assume a 5% annual decline in the base case.
- **PayPal Ads beyond the cherry.** PayPal sees purchase data across hundreds of millions of accounts and millions of merchants, so advertisers can measure actual purchases across many stores rather than one retailer's site. Mark Grether runs PayPal Ads; he previously built Uber's ad business and led product strategy for Amazon's advertising. PayPal launched Transaction Graph Insights and Measurement in January 2026 and an Ads Manager for small merchants. PayPal has not disclosed ad revenue, so I give ads no value outside the Bull + Ads case.

## What could go wrong

- **Branded checkout could start shrinking.** Apple Pay, Shop Pay and AI-agent checkout keep competing for the button, and branded checkout is still about half of TM$.
- **The quality of Q3's profit growth was mixed.** TM$ grew about $79 million, but transaction and credit losses fell by about $97 million (to $386 million from $483 million). In other words, lower losses more than explain the entire increase. Pricing and volume did not drive it, and loss rates can't fall forever.
- **Net income is still declining, and GAAP looks worse than non-GAAP.** Non-GAAP net income is down about 5% in 2026, so today's EPS growth comes entirely from buybacks. On a GAAP basis it is weaker: Q3 GAAP net income fell 14% and GAAP EPS fell 4%, and full-year GAAP EPS is guided to about $5.07, down from $5.41. Management's 2027 outlook implies another year of lower net income. In my base case, net income only just inflects. It needs to grow for the multiple to hold.
- **The buyback depends on adjusted free cash flow.** The 13% yield uses PayPal's adjusted FCF (about $2.0 billion in Q3, $6.5 billion+ for 2026). Reported free cash flow was $1.4 billion in Q3, down 17%. If the gap persists, the buyback engine runs slower than modeled. And a growing credit and BNPL book (the bull case) ties up more balance sheet, which can pull cash conversion down just as growth picks up.
- **A credit downturn would hit BNPL and credit**, which are the fastest-growing profit engines, and would reverse the lower losses that drove Q3.
- **Lores's HP record cuts both ways.** HP's revenue fell from about $58.8 billion to $55.3 billion during his tenure. The per-share playbook works, but at PayPal it has to come with growth.

## Bottom line

At about $57 a share and roughly 10x earnings, the market is pricing PayPal as a business that won't grow. The math says that doesn't need to change for the stock to work:

- **At today's growth rates**, buybacks alone deliver EPS growth in the low teens and roughly a 14% IRR to 2030.
- **If Lores delivers mid-single-digit TM$ growth**, which needs the growth engines to roughly double their pace, the stock is worth about $127 in 2030 at the same multiple, a 22% IRR.
- **What I'm not counting is the margin of safety:** reinvested savings, checkout share gains spreading, a stable rate backdrop, and most of the ads opportunity.

The biggest risk is not missing guidance. It is net income continuing to shrink, which management's own 2027 outlook already implies for one more year, and the market cutting the multiple. The next proof point is PayPal's 2027 guidance in February: what matters is whether it shows TM$ accelerating, not just another EPS beat.
